## 1. What counts as "the simplest job"

Not every carport is equal in build difficulty. The easiest category, and the one we should be
chasing first, has all of these traits:

- **Single-slope lean-to**, attached to an existing wall (house, garage, barn) — one ridge height,
  no ridge beam, every rafter the same length. This is the least material, least design decisions,
  least skill required of any carport style.
- **Single-car footprint**, roughly **12'×20'**, at least 6' high.
- **Flat, already-graded ground** — no excavation, no retaining, no drainage engineering beyond
  standard roof slope.
- **No electrical, no slab, no enclosed walls** — just posts, roof frame, and roofing panels.
- **Small enough to often skip a permit** — many jurisdictions exempt structures under ~200 sq ft
  (a 12'×20' lean-to is right at that line — confirm per city/county before assuming).

This is the job type to actively seek out and price aggressively on. It's also the best training
ground: low stakes if we're slow the first few times, and it directly builds the skills the harder
freestanding jobs need later.

## 2. Where to source cheap kits

### Factory-direct manufacturers
Best combination of price and reliability for a small shop starting out. They sell factory-direct
(no middleman markup) and often include delivery, sometimes installation:
- Alan's Factory Outlet, Carports R Us, Get Carports (authorized dealer network, claims up to 30%
  off local-contractor pricing), Waldrop Metal Buildings, Absolute Steel
- Get carports's authorized-dealer model is worth studying separately — becoming an *authorized
  dealer* ourselves down the line could be cheaper than buying retail kits one at a time

### Big-box stores with online design tools
- Menards, Lowe's, Home Depot all have configurator tools that price a kit to your exact dimensions.
  Useful as a fast, no-phone-call price check even if we don't buy from them — it's a live market
  comparable for whatever we quote a customer.

### Wholesale / import
- Made-in-China, Global Sources, and similar B2B marketplaces show rock-bottom unit pricing, but
  come with shipping lead time, container minimums, and no local recourse if a shipment shows up
  wrong. **Not a fit for single small jobs.** Worth revisiting only once we're buying hardware or
  panel stock in volume for a design we've standardized on (see the product-line section of the
  reverse-engineering guide).

### Marketplace / secondhand / scratch-and-dent
- Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, and local classifieds occasionally list take-down kits (someone
  removing a carport to build a garage) or manufacturer scratch-and-dent stock at a steep discount.
  Slower to find, but the cheapest possible source when it appears — worth a standing saved search.

## 3. Price benchmarks

| Structure type | Typical cost | Source |
|---|---|---|
| DIY wood lean-to, single car, materials only, self-built | **$700–$1,000** | [How to Build a Lean-To Carport](https://alansfactoryoutlet.com/blog/how-to-build-a-lean-to-carport/) |
| Basic metal single-car carport kit | **$1,200–$3,500** | [Metal Carport Kits](https://alansfactoryoutlet.com/carport-kits/) |
| Metal double-car carport kit | **$2,500–$6,000** | same |
| Small carport pole building (fully built, permitted-scale) | **$7,000–$18,000** | [Hansen Pole Buildings](https://www.hansenpolebuildings.com/residential-buildings/carport-buildings/) |

The gap between row 1 and row 2 is the whole point of this guide: **a self-built wood lean-to costs
roughly a third of a comparable metal kit**, because we're paying for lumber and hardware instead of
factory fabrication and a dealer margin. That's our natural price advantage on the simplest job type
— we should be quoting near kit-retail prices while paying closer to raw-material cost.

## 4. Comparing kits apples-to-apples

Two kits at the same sticker price are rarely the same structure. Before comparing price, normalize
for:

- **Panel gauge** — 29 ga vs. 26 ga is a real durability difference, not just a number (see the
  reverse-engineering guide's roofing section)
- **Post spacing and material** — wood post-frame vs. tube steel changes both cost and skill needed
- **What's included** — delivery? Installation? Anchoring hardware? A "cheaper" kit that excludes
  installation may cost more once our labor is added
- **Engineering documentation** — does the kit come with a stamped drawing we can hand to a permit
  office, or are we on our own for that paperwork?

Use the spec sheet template from `reverse-engineering-prefab-kits.md` to record these fields for
every kit we price-check — same format, so a $1,400 kit and a $2,200 kit are actually comparable
before we pick one.

## 5. Red flags — where "cheap" becomes a liability

- **No published gauge or panel spec** — if a listing won't say the steel thickness, assume it's
  the thinnest option on the market.
- **No anchor/footing guidance included** — means the cheap price doesn't include what it takes to
  actually make the structure code-compliant once it's in the ground.
- **Import kits with no US-based support** — fine for a one-time DIY buyer, risky for a business
  reselling installs, since we own the customer relationship if something arrives damaged or wrong.
- **Price that undercuts the DIY-materials benchmark in §3** — if a finished kit is cheaper than raw
  lumber and steel would cost us, something is being cut (gauge, hardware quality, or it's a
  bait price with mandatory costly add-ons at checkout).

## 6. Our starting strategy

1. Take the **simple lean-to jobs first** — lowest material cost, fastest build, least permit
   friction, and the best way to build a photo portfolio fast (see the earlier business-model notes
   on before/after photos being our best marketing asset).
2. Price every job against both a **self-built wood estimate** and a **factory kit quote** — quote
   near the kit price, build at closer to materials cost, and that spread is our margin while we're
   still small.
3. Save a standing search on marketplace/classifieds sites for scratch-and-dent or take-down kits —
   free money if one turns up before a job that fits its dimensions.
4. Once we've done a handful of simple lean-tos and have our own standard spec (from the
   reverse-engineering process), start pricing wholesale/bulk hardware and panel stock instead of
   buying kit-by-kit — that's the next cost step-down.

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] Confirmed the job is single-slope, attached, ~12'×20' or smaller — the "simple" category
- [ ] Checked local permit threshold (often ~200 sq ft) before assuming no-permit
- [ ] Got a self-build materials estimate (lumber + hardware + roofing)
- [ ] Got at least one factory-direct kit quote for the same footprint as a price comparable
- [ ] Normalized both against gauge, post spacing, and what's included (delivery/install/engineering)
- [ ] Checked marketplace/classifieds for a scratch-and-dent option before buying new
- [ ] Verified nothing in the cheap quote is missing anchor hardware, footing spec, or documentation
- [ ] Logged the kit on the shared spec sheet template for future comparison
