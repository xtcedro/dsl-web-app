# In-Person Marketing Guide

*Two separate skills, both required: finding the right people to talk to, and knowing what to say
once you're standing in front of them.*

## Part one: identifying the right people

Untargeted canvassing wastes more time than it saves. The goal is to spend in-person effort only on
households and partners who are unusually likely to say yes.

### A. Read the property, not the neighborhood

Drive for signal, not just streets. The tells that a household is a strong prospect:

- **No carport or garage visible**, especially older ranch-style homes common across OKC-metro
  neighborhoods built before covered parking was standard.
- **A vehicle, RV, or boat parked in the open** — visible proof they already own something worth
  covering and don't currently have a place to put it.
- **Gravel or dirt parking area** instead of a paved driveway with covered parking — often signals
  the household has already worked around not having a carport, rather than not wanting one.
- **Rural/acreage properties** — equipment, trucks, and outbuildings are common, and covered parking
  is both more valued and less permit-encumbered than in a dense subdivision.

### B. Screen out low-probability HOA neighborhoods

Some HOAs restrict or ban freestanding carports outright. Rather than discover this mid-pitch,
identify HOA-governed neighborhoods ahead of time and either skip them for cold outreach or adjust
the pitch to attached/architecturally-matched designs that are more likely to be HOA-compliant.

### C. Referral partners — higher yield than cold canvassing

A single relationship can produce leads for months, unlike a one-time knock:

- **Realtors**, especially ones who list rural/acreage or older homes without garages — a carport
  is a low-cost way for a seller to add curb appeal and a buyer amenity. Approach with something
  useful to *them*, not a sales pitch: e.g., offering a fast quote turnaround for their sellers
  prepping a listing, not asking them to "refer us."
- **Fence companies, roofers, and general contractors** — genuinely complementary, not competing,
  trades. A homeowner getting a new fence or roof is already in a home-improvement mindset. Set up
  a two-way referral arrangement: send them carport customers who ask about fencing/roofing, they
  send us customers who mention wanting covered parking.
- **Local networking groups** (e.g., BNI-style referral groups) — built specifically around
  cross-trade referral relationships; members report meaningful annual referral volume once
  established, though it takes consistent attendance to pay off, not a single visit.

### D. Your own finished jobs are a prospecting list

The moment a job is done and photographed, the surrounding block is now a warm list — neighbors who
watched the build happen are pre-sold on the idea in a way a cold prospect never is. Canvass the
immediate neighborhood within days of finishing a visible job, while the structure is still fresh
in view.

### E. Home & garden shows

The **Oklahoma City Home + Garden Show** at OKC Fair Park runs annually (late Jan/early Feb) with
300+ home-project vendors. Booth packages start around **$100–$200 for a new-exhibitor package**.
This is less about closing deals on the spot and more about high-volume, low-cost lead capture —
bring the portfolio and a simple way to collect contact info (QR code to the quote form is ideal).

## Part two: presenting in person

### A. First impressions are fast — and mostly about trust, not persuasion

You have roughly 7 seconds to read as legitimate rather than as a nuisance. This isn't about being
slick — it's:

- Professional appearance and a company-branded shirt or truck signage if available
- Knock/ring, then step back — never crowd the doorway
- Lead with identification, not a pitch: who you are, what company, why you're there, in one
  sentence

### B. Ask before you offer

The instinct is to launch straight into features. Resist it. Open with a question that surfaces
what they actually want: *"I noticed you don't have covered parking — is that something you've
thought about, or is the driveway working fine for you?"* Their answer tells you which parts of the
pitch matter and which to skip entirely.

### C. Lead with the portfolio, not the pitch

Photos of finished local work do more work than any script. Have the before/after portfolio
(built per `marketing-and-lead-gen-playbook.md`) ready on a phone or tablet, ideally showing a job
similar in style to their property. Seeing a real, nearby, finished structure converts skepticism
into interest faster than describing one.

### D. Build credibility signals into the conversation naturally

Mention — don't recite — the things that separate a legitimate small operation from someone just
going door to door with no backing:

- Licensed/registered with the city (per `okc-metro-permit-guide.md`)
- Carries general liability insurance
- Can walk through the permit process for their specific city
- Warranty terms on workmanship

This isn't a compliance checklist to read aloud — it's material that answers the objections a
homeowner is silently forming before they ask.

### E. Handle objections calmly, don't argue them down

Common ones and how to meet them:

- *"I need to think about it"* → Not a rejection — offer to leave a written quote and follow up on
  a specific day, rather than pushing for a decision on the spot.
- *"How do I know you'll actually finish it"* → This is where the portfolio, the registration, and
  the insurance certificate do the talking — reassurance through specifics, not reassurance through
  insistence.
- *"That seems expensive"* → Walk the price apart (materials, labor, permit) using the same
  breakdown from `pricing-and-quoting.md` rather than just discounting — a customer who understands
  what they're paying for negotiates less than one who thinks the number is arbitrary.

### F. Closing without pressure

The strongest closers in this trade close by being calm and clear about the process, not by
creating urgency. Explain what happens after a yes: deposit, permit, start date, what the crew needs
from them on build day. When a customer can picture the whole process, the decision gets easier —
that's the actual close, not a scripted pressure line.

## 3. Compliance before you knock on anything

Door-to-door sales are regulated **city by city** across the OKC metro, separate from contractor
registration:

- Oklahoma City requires an **Outdoor Seller/Peddler/Solicitor's License** to sell door-to-door,
  which includes proof of sales tax registration with the Oklahoma Tax Commission.
- Norman has its own **Solicitor's/Peddler's License** application and requirements.
- Other metro cities (Mustang, Del City, and others) have their own peddler/solicitor ordinances.

**Confirm and obtain the correct license for each city before canvassing there** — this is separate
from, and in addition to, the contractor registration covered in `okc-metro-permit-guide.md`. Don't
assume a license in one city carries over to the next.

If a quote is closed inside the customer's home rather than at a fixed place of business, the FTC's
3-day cancellation right likely applies — see `quote-contract-template.md` §4. In-person selling is
exactly the scenario that rule was written for, so treat it as standard practice, not an edge case.

## 4. What to carry

- Business cards and a one-page leave-behind with photos and a QR code to the site's quote form
- Portfolio on a phone/tablet, organized by job style so you can pull up something similar fast
- Proof of insurance / registration (or a card referencing where to verify it)
- A simple written-quote pad — leaving something tangible beats a verbal promise
- Weather basics if canvassing on foot: water, sun protection in summer

## 5. Tracking so nothing falls through

Cold outreach dies from bad follow-up, not bad pitches. Keep a simple log (spreadsheet is fine at
this scale) per address/contact:

```
Address | Date visited | Outcome (not home / not interested / interested / quoted) |
Follow-up date | Notes
```

Follow up on a specific promised date — "I'll check back Thursday" and actually doing it is a
meaningful trust signal on its own.

## 6. Checklist

- [ ] Identified target neighborhoods using the property signals in §A, not just proximity
- [ ] Confirmed HOA status before investing time in a cold pitch there
- [ ] Reached out to at least one complementary trade (fence, roofing, realtor) about a referral
      arrangement
- [ ] Canvassed the block around every finished job within days of completion
- [ ] Obtained the correct peddler/solicitor license for every city being canvassed
- [ ] Portfolio loaded and organized on a phone/tablet before going out
- [ ] Written-quote pad and business cards on hand
- [ ] Follow-up log set up and actually used after every visit, not just the promising ones
